One world,
rival theories

A world, a war, and the question it left behind. Then four theories, each looking at the same world and keeping what it needs to explain. Jack Snyder gave the title (2004).

A world

Four states, and what happened to them.

    Why theory

    To prevent the next war, you need to know what caused the last one. History gives the facts: the arms, the alliances, the dates. It does not say which of them mattered. A theory does that. It picks the few that matter and draws the arrows between them, so that we can act on them. Different theories pick different facts. That is this course.

    What a theory is

    History gives us facts. To understand them we need explanation, and explanation needs theory.

    A definition

    • A theory is a logically consistent set of statements that explains a phenomenon of interest.
    • It has three parts: assumptions, the logical connections between them, and the implications that follow (Bueno de Mesquita 2013).
    • Its claims are probabilistic: factors that make an outcome more or less likely, not certain.

    Lecture 3, slides 4 and 5

    What it does

    • Explain: find causes and link them. The main job.
    • Describe: pick out which events matter. A theory of trade tells you what to look at before a trade war.
    • Predict: if the causes change, how do outcomes change?
    • Prescribe: suggest policy, for example ways to help states cooperate.
    • No single theory answers every puzzle. “One of the principal contributions that international relations theory can make is not predicting the future but providing the vocabulary and conceptual framework to ask hard questions” (Snyder 2004).

    Lecture 3, slide 5. Lecture 4, slide 3

    Is it science?

    • “Every time I wash my car, it rains.” A pattern is not an explanation.
    • Falsifiability (Popper): a scientific theory says what evidence would prove it wrong, and stands until it is falsified.
    • Dependent variable: what we want to explain. Independent variable: what we think explains it.
    • Explanatory theories ask why something happened. Normative ones ask how the world ought to be. Critical and constitutive theories ask who the order serves and how it is made possible at all, and reject causal theorizing.
    • Words you will meet: paradigm (a school’s basic assumptions and concepts), ontology (what exists), epistemology (how we know), methodology (how we study it).

    Four theories, one world

    The same four states, seen through four theories. Each starts from its own assumptions and keeps the facts that fit them.

      How the field argued with itself

      Four “great debates”, a simplification of a messier history. The schools below took shape inside them.

      First · 1920s to 1950sRealism vs idealism

      Can law and institutions tame power politics? Idealists had Wilson’s Fourteen Points (1918), the League of Nations and the Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928). Realists answered with Carr, The Twenty Years’ Crisis (1939), and Morgenthau, Politics Among Nations (1948). The 1930s seemed to prove them right. Realism won, but liberalism did not disappear.

      Lecture 3, slides 8 and 11

      Second · 1950s and 1960sTraditionalism vs behaviouralism

      A debate about method: can IR be a science? Traditionalists (historians, lawyers, former diplomats) used history, analogy and philosophy. Behaviouralists used hypotheses, data and statistics, as in the Correlates of War project (Singer and Small, from 1963). Today both count. What matters is rigor.

      Lecture 3, slides 12, 16 and 17

      Third · 1980sNeorealism vs neoliberalism

      The “neo-neo” debate, between two rationalist, state-centric camps that share the same picture of anarchy. The question: can institutions help states overcome cheating and the fear of relative gains? Waltz (1979) and Grieco on one side, Keohane (1984) on the other.

      Lecture 3, slide 18. Lecture 5, slides 2 and 16

      Fourth · 1990s onwardRationalism vs reflectivism

      A debate about knowledge: explaining or understanding? Keohane named the two sides in his 1988 address to the International Studies Association. Reflectivists (constructivists, feminists, critical theorists) reject the split between facts and values and study meanings, norms and history. Some constructivists claim a middle ground.

      Lecture 3, slides 18 and 19. Lecture 5, slides 2 and 19

      The founding story says IR began in 1919, at Aberystwyth, to find the causes of war. The role of race in early IR complicates that story: see Critical approaches below. Lecture 3, slides 8 to 10.

      RealismPower and survival under anarchy

      What it sees: states, unequal in power, with no authority above them. The stronger grows and the other fears. Trade and treaties are in the background.

      Assumptions

      • States are the main actors, and great powers matter most.
      • Anarchy: no legitimate authority above states.
      • The state is a unitary actor, a “black box” that speaks with one voice whatever its regime or leaders.
      • Survival comes first, pursued through self-help.
      • Mearsheimer adds: every state has some offensive capability, intentions can never be known for certain, and states are rational. The result is fear and power maximization, “the tragedy of great power politics”.

      Lecture 4, slides 7 and 8 (Mearsheimer 2013, 78 to 80)

      Concepts

      • Power: hard (military), latent (wealth and population), soft (attraction).
      • Security dilemma: steps one state takes for its own security make others less secure, even when both want peace (Jervis 1976).
      • Polarity: unipolar, bipolar, multipolar.
      • Balancing, bandwagoning, buck-passing.
      • Relative gains: what matters is how much I gain compared with you. So cooperation is hard, and institutions mostly reflect the distribution of power (Mearsheimer, “The False Promise of International Institutions”).

      Lecture 4, slides 9, 10, 15, 18, 20 and 21. Lecture 5, slide 16

      Varieties and roots

      • Classical: human nature drives conflict (Morgenthau 1948). Structural: the anarchic system does (Waltz 1979).
      • Defensive: states maximize security. Offensive: states maximize power (Mearsheimer). Neoclassical: domestic factors intervene.
      • Roots: Thucydides (“the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must”), Hobbes (no sovereign above sovereigns), Machiavelli (the ruler’s first duty is the survival of the state).
      • In our readings: Brooks and Wohlforth on polarity (Week 2). Allison’s Thucydides Trap and Poast on the security dilemma (Week 4). Wakanda hiding its wealth as defensive realism (Beauchamp 2018, Week 5).

      Lecture 4, slides 6, 11, 14 and 19

      LiberalismCooperation is hard, not impossible

      What it sees: the same states, joined by trade and a shared institution. Cheating is tempting, but they will meet again next year.

      Assumptions

      • Humans can reason and make progress. All liberals assume “the possibility of cumulative progress in human affairs” (Sterling-Folker 2013: 115).
      • Kant’s Perpetual Peace gives the three branches: republican government (democratic peace), commerce (interdependence), and law and organization (neoliberal institutionalism).
      • Neoliberals share realism’s picture: central, unitary, rational states under anarchy. They “sought to challenge realist pessimism on its own terms” (Sterling-Folker 2013: 115–116).
      • The difference: for realists anarchy is a constant, for neoliberals a vacuum that institutions can fill. States care about absolute gains: am I better off than before?

      Lecture 5, slides 3 to 5 and 9 to 11

      Concepts

      • Free-riding: “to fail to contribute to a public good while benefiting from the contributions of others.”
      • Transaction costs: the time, effort and resources needed to negotiate, monitor and enforce deals.
      • Prisoner’s Dilemma: each side’s incentive to defect leaves both worse off. Neoliberals ask: why assume only one round? Repeated interaction makes cheating today costly.
      • Institutions supply obligation, information, monitoring and predictability, so anarchy matters less.
      • Regimes: “principles, norms, rules and decision-making procedures around which actor expectations converge in a given issue-area” (Krasner 1982: 185).

      Lecture 5, slides 11 to 15

      Key works and readings

      • Keohane and Nye, Power and Interdependence (1977). Krasner (ed.), International Regimes (1983). Keohane, After Hegemony (1984): cooperation can outlast the hegemon that built the institutions.
      • The setting: European integration, the GATT, the 1973 oil crisis.
      • In our readings: the liberal international order (Ikenberry 2020, Danforth 2026, Week 1). Nordhaus, “The Climate Club” (2020, Week 5): voluntary climate deals invite free-riding, and a club that penalizes outsiders changes the incentives.

      Lecture 5, slides 7 and 9

      ConstructivismIdeas give power its meaning

      What it sees: the same armies, but one neighbour is called a friend and the other a rival. That word, not the army, decides who is afraid.

      Claims

      • Ideas constitute: shared ideas give material facts their meaning. “The state is a state of mind, not a material object.”
      • Agents and structures make each other. Neither comes first.
      • Identities shape interests. Interests are not given in advance, they are formed.
      • “Not that material conditions no longer matter. It is that how they matter depends on what they mean” (Finnemore and Wendt).
      • Two logics: of consequences (which choice gives the best result?) and of appropriateness (what is the right thing for an actor like me to do here?).

      Lecture 5, slides 19 and 23

      Three pictures

      • 500 against 5: “500 British nuclear weapons are less threatening to the United States than 5 North Korean nuclear weapons” (Wendt 1995: 73). A security dilemma and a security community are both social structures. One rests on distrust, the other on trust.
      • Anarchy is what states make of it (Wendt 1992): a state that identifies only with itself lives in realist anarchy. One that identifies with others does not. Hopf: “a continuum of anarchies is possible.” Why is there no security dilemma between the US and Canada?
      • Fire in a theatre (Hopf 1998: 173): one exit, everyone runs. Who goes first? Material power cannot answer. You need the “culture, norms, institutions, procedures, rules, and social practices” of the people inside.

      Lecture 5, slides 20, 21 and 24

      Why it rose, and readings

      • Nicholas Onuf introduced the term (1989). The approach took off from 1989 to 1999.
      • The Cold War ended when the USSR dissolved itself, which the mainstream theories had not seen as possible. Global civil society, NGOs and human rights campaigns needed explaining too.
      • In our readings: social construction, one of Walt’s five concepts (Week 3). Nakia and T’Challa open Wakanda to change how the world sees it (Beauchamp 2018, Week 5).

      Lecture 5, slide 22

      FeminismWhere are the women?

      What it sees: who sits at the table and who is out of frame. The work that keeps states going, at home, on bases and in factories, is done where the other theories do not look.

      Claims

      • Feminism is “the study of and movement for women not as objects but as subjects of knowledge.” In IR since the late 1980s, starting from the question “where are the women?”
      • Sex is biological. Gender is social: “socially learned behavior and expectations that distinguish between masculinity and femininity.”
      • Gender is relational, “a primary way of signifying relationships of power” (Scott 1986). For most feminists it is an analytical tool, not only a description.
      • It challenges state-centrism.

      Lecture 5, slides 26 and 27

      Concepts

      • Gendered pairs. Masculine: power, autonomy, rationality, strength, public. Feminine: nature, emotion, passivity, private. The pairs produce a hierarchy.
      • In foreign policy, compromise reads as weakness: “standing tall rather than wimping out.”
      • Liberal feminism removes legal obstacles and “adds women”. Post-liberal feminisms (Marxist, socialist, radical, standpoint, postcolonial, postmodern) study the deep structures of patriarchy.

      Lecture 5, slides 26 and 27, and notes

      What feminists study, and readings

      • The gendered nature of war and peace, of development, and of the global division of labour. Potentially “discipline- and world-transforming” (Sjoberg and Tickner).
      • Cynthia Enloe, Bananas, Beaches and Bases (1989). J. Ann Tickner, Gender in International Relations (1992).
      • In our readings: Sjoberg (2014), “‘Mansplaining’ International Relations? What Walt Misses” (Week 3). Beauchamp’s reading of Black Panther barely mentions gender. What would a feminist reading add? (Week 5)

      Lecture 5, slides 28 and 29

      Critical approachesWho does the order serve?

      What it sees: not anarchy among equals but a hierarchy. Some states were made by ruling others, and wealth still flows one way.

      Claims

      • Critical theory exposes domination and structural inequalities of power, and seeks emancipation. Postcolonialism is one example.
      • Postpositivist: the observer and the observed cannot be separated, because theories are shaped by their time and culture.
      • The world is a hierarchy as much as an anarchy. Sjoberg (Week 3): some states and people count more than others.

      Lecture 3, slide 3 and notes

      Race and the origins of IR

      • The Journal of Race Development (1910 to 1919) became the Journal of International Relations (1919 to 1922), then Foreign Affairs (1922 onward). That complicates the founding myth that IR began only to prevent another world war.
      • The Howard School: Black IR thinkers around Howard University in the 1930s and 1940s, Alain Locke, Ralph Bunche and Merze Tate, with race and racism as central forces in world politics. Beauchamp calls Tate the first Black woman to receive a PhD in international relations.

      Lecture 3, slide 10. Beauchamp 2018

      Tate’s warning, and readings

      • Tate (1943): an unjust world order is unstable, because the oppressed see the injustice and will not accept it forever. Western thinkers “think and write entirely too much in terms of saving European civilization, ignoring the fact that that civilization is a partial and secondary culture serving a minority of the peoples of the world.”
      • In our readings: a “long peace” among Europe’s great powers, 1815 to 1914, alongside many colonial wars. Peace for whom? (Week 2). Beauchamp reads Killmonger as “Tate’s warning brought to life” (Week 5).
